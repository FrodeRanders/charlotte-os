\chapter{Endpoints: Communication as a First-Class Primitive}
\label{chap:endpoints}

An endpoint answers: \textbf{``Who am I communicating with?''} It is
CharlotteOS' primary primitive for calls between protection domains.

\section{Server and connection model}

An \textbf{endpoint} is a bounded server-side IPC receive object. A service
creates one through which it receives messages. A \textbf{connection capability}
is a client-side authority to send to that endpoint. Connections are minted
from the endpoint (typically by the name service) and delegated to clients.

\begin{verbatim}
Service creates endpoint:   endpoint_create(interface="ns", capacity=16)
Name service mints conn:    connection_mint(endpoint, SEND | CALL)
Client receives conn:       name_service.lookup("ns") -> ConnectionCap
\end{verbatim}

Ordinary clients do not mint arbitrary connections. The name service or a policy
service normally delegates them after an access check.

\begin{architecturalcomparison}{Xous messages and an asynchronous caller}
Xous supplies the direct precedent for isolated servers and distinct scalar,
owned-memory and borrowed-memory messages. In its documented IPC interface,
blocking scalar calls and memory borrows suspend the calling thread until the
server responds or returns the memory \cite{prior:xous}. CharlotteOS makes
a distinction between sending a small control message and transferring or
lending page-backed data. However, it represents an outstanding call with an
owned \texttt{PendingCall} and gives the server a one-shot \texttt{ReplyToken}.

This change serves the documented Sitas co-design: one shard can keep other
tasks running while a call is pending, then park its thread when no task can
progress. A borrow remains live through the pending owner, so suspension of a
Rust task does not return its memory. The cost is an explicit call lifecycle,
including cancellation and returned-capability cleanup, rather than relying
on one blocked stack to delimit the interaction. See the
\repofiletext|docs/architecture/sitas-xous.md|{co-design note} and
\autoref{sec:reconnecting-client}.
\end{architecturalcomparison}

\subsection{Namespace publication}

An IPC namespace belongs to one exact root generation. It must be prepared
before ordinary IPC can use it, and retirement prevents new publication into it.

\begin{implementationdetail}{Namespace publication and retirement}
Root registration prepares the IPC namespace node and both budget accounts
fallibly before taking publication guards. It captures an exact-generation
root lease, releases lifecycle/table guards, then links that prepared storage
under IPC alone. Cleanup cannot pass the retained lease during this handoff.
Missing or retired namespaces reject, and ordinary IPC never recreates them.

After fenced loan and capability drain, cleanup detaches the empty namespace
node and disposes it outside IPC. Outstanding delegated endpoint/record charges
keep their original accounts through ASID reuse. This covers namespace storage, but
guarded per-capability payload factories, account byte/principal
admission and wider destructor contexts remain audit work. See
\repofile|docs/reference/ipc-namespace-publication.md|.
\end{implementationdetail}

\subsection{Endpoint storage admission}

Endpoint records and queue backing have generation-scoped sponsorship. The
current kernel admits at most 64 endpoint records and 8,192 queue slots per
domain, and 1,024 records and 32,768 slots per node. Ordinary domains share
768 records and 24,576 slots, while the remainder is available to kernel-designated
platform domains. These are initial kernel policies, independent of socket
capacity, rather than deployment-descriptor settings.

\paragraph*{Create the queue.}

Creation admits the endpoint record and queue capacity before publishing authority.
A queue admits at most 4,096 messages.

\begin{implementationdetail}{Fallible endpoint preparation}
A single queue admits at most 4,096 messages. Creation keeps its captured
root, reserves record and slot counts atomically, then prepares endpoint storage,
queue backing and observer lists fallibly outside IPC. Slots round up to a
power of two with a minimum of four. Revalidation precedes authority publication
and allocation-free endpoint relinking. Ordinary unused preparation finishes
after local guards, root last, but abandonment retains every field.
\end{implementationdetail}

\paragraph*{Resize with temporary headroom.}

Growth keeps that exact root and the original endpoint sponsor and
classification, then reserves and allocates a second queue outside IPC. Both
backings count at the temporary peak. Revalidation checks root, capability,
endpoint, closing state and available backing before moving messages in FIFO
order. A competing larger resize can make private preparation unnecessary, while
concurrent close rejects it. Replaced or unused backing frees after IPC unlock,
before its charge returns. Promotion cannot reclassify the original endpoint.
Shrinking reduces message admission and keeps backing and its charge.

\paragraph*{Release after the last reference.}

Closing the owner drains the queue after confirmed cleanup. Delegated or queued
connections can keep a closed endpoint record alive, so its admission is returned
only after the remaining storage has been freed.

\begin{implementationdetail}{Endpoint retirement and metadata publication}
Owner closure drains the queue and frees its backing after confirmed cleanup.
Delegated, queued and unobserved returned connections can keep a closed
endpoint. Final-reference removal detaches its complete node into the existing
cleanup owner for post-IPC disposal. Record admission returns after actual
payload and node free. Late cleanup after ASID reuse credits the original
account. Fallback retains the complete node, without cleanup or replay
authority. Endpoint shared-authority metadata now prepares outside IPC.
Count admission revalidates the captured namespace under IPC, then payload
allocation leaves the guard again. Publication relinks prepared storage. Linear
scans and sustained pressure/IRQ hold time remain unqualified. See
\repofile|docs/reference/endpoint-budgets.md|.
\end{implementationdetail}

\subsection{Connection, call and reply record admission}

\paragraph*{Choose the sponsor.}

Each sponsoring namespace generation can hold 512 connection capabilities,
512 pending calls and 512 outstanding reply tokens. Each dimension has a node
ceiling of 8,192 and an ordinary-domain share of 6,144. These initial kernel
policies are separate from endpoint queue capacity and waiter limits.

The grantor sponsors direct grants and re-delegations. The requesting caller
sponsors both its pending call and the server's reply token, and pays for a
connection returned in that solicited reply. Repeated lookups therefore cannot
consume the serving grantor's connection allowance. A recipient can hold
connections sponsored by several namespaces, so 512 is not its total
capability-table limit. Sponsorship does not change capability authority.
Completed and observed calls remain charged until closed, while reply-token
charges return on reply or cancellation.

\paragraph*{Admit before consuming inputs.}

Connection-bearing calls and replies admit their
connection before copying memory, consuming the token or revoking a loan.
Kernel rejection leaves the inputs intact. The consuming Rust
\texttt{ReplyToken::\allowbreak reply\_connection*} methods close/cancel their token on an
error and preserve the borrowed grant source, but they do not return a retryable
token.

\begin{implementationdetail}{Call record preparation and rollback}
Calls reject invalid handles and unavailable queues before preparing record
storage, then recheck them before mutation. They keep their exact caller root
and reserve both record counts atomically
before preparing record storage, waiter metadata or attachments. Pending and
reply-token nodes allocate fallibly outside IPC and publish by relinking. Their containing
owner explicitly finishes unused preparation after IPC unlock, root last, but
abandonment retains every field. Reply, close and confirmed cancellation carry detached record nodes outside IPC
and free payload/storage before refunding the original record charge. Scalar
completion keeps its detached notification batch with the node until explicit
finish, and fallback retains both. Claim sources use the original token
classification through promotion and ASID reuse. See \repofile|docs/reference/ipc-record-budgets.md|.

Call preparation uses the same three-phase authority boundary with its existing
root held, and pending waiter-list construction also leaves IPC. Call staging
only revalidates prepared metadata. Shared quota rejects before payload/list
allocation. Unused shared preparation disposes after local guards, root last, but
abandonment retains the complete prefix. See
\repofile|docs/reference/ipc-capability-storage.md|.
\end{implementationdetail}

\paragraph*{Keep admission through teardown.}

Retirement fences new capability publication before taking the cleanup snapshot.
Existing records keep their original sponsor through delegation and ASID reuse, so
removing authority does not by itself refund allocated storage.

\begin{implementationdetail}{Capability publication and teardown}
Teardown fences admission before taking its capability snapshot. Receive and
connection-publication paths reject a retiring namespace, preventing late
capabilities from escaping that snapshot. Charges stay with the original
generation after delegation or ASID reuse. Per-capability payload nodes now
prepare fallibly after shared count admission and before authority publication.
Installation only relinks prepared storage. Existing owners keep unused
preparation and detached payload/authority pairs through post-IPC disposal.
Connection counts return after actual payload/node free. Speculative receive
rollback keeps queue/token/loans intact and disposes only its reply capability.
Complete fallback retains metadata and charges. See
\repofile|docs/reference/ipc-capability-storage.md|.
\end{implementationdetail}

\begin{implementationdetail}{Payload-node backing budgets}
Capability, endpoint, pending-call and reply-token payload nodes reserve their
requested heap layouts before allocation from \texttt{ipc\_nodes}. The same node
holds backing beside its optional logical record reservation through actual
deallocation. Capability backing borrows the recipient authority namespace's
captured storage class, while grantor/requester logical sponsorship stays independent.
Endpoint/call/reply backing uses its original resource reservation class.
The expanded pool allows 12/16 MiB and 73728/98304 ordinary/total nodes, sized for
all four layouts while preserving existing logical ceilings. The current
24-pool inventory uses statistics version 11, including separate store-image
admission, but header/record widths and runtime ABI remain unchanged. Complete containing
operations and detached lists retain both charges under unknown guards. Nested
buffers and broader heap/principal admission remain separate. See
\repofile|docs/reference/ipc-node-admission.md|.
\end{implementationdetail}

\begin{implementationdetail}{Attachment and cleanup buffer admission}
Attachment retirement catalogs also reserve exact capacity from that pool's
byte dimension, with zero node slots and the original requester class. A
136-byte retirement entry gives a maximum 255-entry catalog of 34680 bytes.
Call rejection and successful receive keep this storage in their complete owner
until explicit disposal after IPC unlock. FIFO child handoff preserves full
capacity and charge through every callback, and interruption retains remaining
owners. Buffer deallocation precedes refund. The inline reservation adds 24 bytes
per queued-message payload slot.

Parsed-descriptor, capability-ID,
transfer-staging and reply-token loan vectors share byte-only admission through
an allocator carrying the original class. Growth admits the full candidate while
old capacity remains charged, and pop/clear keep capacity until deallocation.
The capability-vector allocator adds another 8 inline bytes per message slot.

Descriptor snapshots also reserve 4082 requested bytes through the captured IPC
allocator, before parsing or preparing transfers. Quota rejection returns
\texttt{ResourceLimit} and preserves the descriptor and source, but successful
submission follows its existing descriptor-consumption rule. The byte charge
ends after actual temporary-buffer disposal, before submission roots complete.

Reply/cancellation work also shares byte admission, cloning the accepted token's
allocator class. Each entry is 200 bytes, so 255 entries request 51000 bytes. Empty
work allocates nothing. Capacity survives the final pop and is freed after child
cleanup, before root completion. Complete parents retain returned-memory escrow,
detached storage and notifications on abandonment. Quota rejection precedes the
claim and preserves accepted authority. Cleanup headroom is not reserved at call
acceptance, so saturation can postpone cleanup. No numerical defaults change, and
general heap/principal admission and integrated progress qualification remain open.
\end{implementationdetail}

\begin{implementationdetail}{Mixed capability and memory publication}
Direct-grant, receive and returned-connection factories still run under IPC.
The memory/authority gate now borrows its existing reservations/transfers and
validates the complete set before mutation under one capability hold, without
a temporary vector. Complete prepared scalar call commit can publish, install
admitted nodes and enqueue with heap held. Global memory-object records now
also relink prepared storage, with identities claimed before publication.
Target memory-capability, object and read-loan maps relink prepared admitted
nodes after authority publication under the same registry hold. Complete mixed
commit is heap-free in the documented maximum-batch control, but preparation and
outer disposal remain separate contexts. See \repofile|docs/reference/ipc-publication-batches.md|.
Other capability kinds, byte/principal admission, wider contexts and
comprehensive heap accounting remain incomplete.
\end{implementationdetail}

\subsection{Receive and reply waiter admission}

\paragraph*{Register before parking.}

Threads waiting for endpoint readiness or a pending-call reply use removable,
generation-sponsored registrations. Each source links at most 64 entries. They share
the completion/CQ waiter pools: 1,024 entries per waiting domain, 8,192 per node
and an ordinary share of 6,144. Sponsorship follows the waiting thread, not the
endpoint owner. Admission precedes scheduler parking, but message or reply state
that is already ready returns without keeping an entry.

\paragraph*{Cancel the registration when waiting ends.}

Ready admission or thread reaping cancels the owning registration. IPC detaches
message, reply and source-closure notifications under its registry lock and
invokes them only after releasing that lock. Endpoint closure can combine
receiver and queued-call batches without allocating a callback vector. Entries
remain charged until their detached storage is actually freed.
Detached batches no longer occupy the source's linked count, but they still occupy
the shared domain/node pools while a reusable readiness list is rearmed.

\paragraph*{Keep the call and loan through rejected waits.}

Untimed receive and reply waits retry cooperatively when admission is full. A
reply wait preserves the pending call and its memory loan until reply or explicit
cancellation revokes receiver access. The existing Rust owners need no manual
unregister step. Kernel timed reply waits can return without a reply on deadline
or admission rejection, and neither event by itself ends a loan.

\begin{implementationdetail}{Waiter-list preparation}
Readiness lists and pending-call lists are prepared fallibly before source
publication. Call preparation precedes memory transfer, lending and delegated
connection attachments, so failure at this stage leaves those inputs untouched.
Connection, reply-token and pending-call counts use the separate record budgets
above. List control blocks and other kernel metadata still need comprehensive
admission and fallible allocation.
\end{implementationdetail}

\section{Message classes}

The ABI distinguishes two message classes:

\subsection{Scalar messages}

A scalar message carries an opcode, one 64-bit argument word, and optionally a
reply token. It fits in registers. It is suitable for:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Opcodes and request types.
    \item Capability handles.
    \item Status values and queue indices.
    \item Compact control-plane operations where the payload is a single word.
\end{itemize}

Scalar messages require no memory-object allocation, page-table change, or TLB
invalidation.

\subsection{Memory-based messages}

A message can carry one or more \textbf{memory-object attachments} with explicit
transfer modes (see \autoref{chap:memory-objects}). These are suitable for:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Structured requests and responses.
    \item Strings, names, and configuration structures.
    \item Packet payloads and block I/O buffers.
    \item Larger replies that exceed register width.
\end{itemize}

The kernel validates each attachment: the sender must hold the referenced memory
object with sufficient rights for the declared transfer mode. The receiver gets a
new capability to the memory (or a temporary mapping, for borrows).

Scalar copy/move sends and copy/move/read/write-loan calls capture both exact
roots and prepare memory outside IPC, then revalidate the captured connection,
namespaces and endpoint before publication. A single send owns its transfer,
attachment storage and pending delivery in one containing owner. Explicit finish
runs after IPC unlock, notifies before completing the roots, and restores an
unpublished source or releases private copy backing on ordinary rejection.
Complete single/vector-send and claimed-reply parents retain all fields on
abandonment, including returned-memory escrow and work capacity. Explicit known
unstarted rejection still restores its preparation. Other active transfer
parents and enclosing IRQ contexts require separate qualification.

\section{Message envelope}

The kernel validates a fixed-width message envelope. It does not understand Rust
enums, \texttt{serde} formats, or protocol-specific types:

\begin{lstlisting}[style=rustCode]
struct MessageHeader {
    interface: u128,       // protocol identity
    version: u32,
    opcode: u32,
    flags: u32,
    inline_len: u16,
    segment_count: u16,
    capability_count: u16,
    reserved: u16,
    user_tag: u64,
}
\end{lstlisting}

Constraints on the ABI:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Fixed-width fields: no \texttt{usize}, no pointer-sized values.
    \item Explicit alignment: no compiler-dependent layout.
    \item Checked offsets and lengths: the kernel rejects out-of-bounds.
    \item Explicit protocol versioning: forward compatibility is negotiated.
    \item Maximum attachment counts: bounded kernel work per message.
    \item No arbitrary userspace pointers as durable message identity.
\end{itemize}

\section{Call and reply}

The most common IPC pattern is \textbf{call/reply}: a client sends a request and
expects a response.

\subsection{Synchronous-looking futures}

At the Rust level, a client writes:


\begin{lstlisting}[style=rustCode]
let response = network_service.connect(address).await?;
\end{lstlisting}

Underneath, the runtime:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item Sends a call message through the connection capability.
    \item The kernel creates a one-shot \textbf{reply token} bound to this call.
    \item The caller task becomes pending (the \texttt{Future} returns
        \texttt{Poll::Pending}).
    \item The caller shard continues running other tasks: no thread is
        dedicated to this request.
    \item The server receives the message on its endpoint.
    \item The server replies (immediately or later).
    \item Reply arrival wakes the client future, which returns
        \texttt{Poll::Ready(response)}.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Deferred replies}

A server may keep a reply token and complete it later while continuing to
serve other requests on the same shard. This supports device-driven
operations:


\begin{lstlisting}[style=rustCode]
async fn handle_read(pending: PendingRequest) {
    // Submit a DMA read, retaining the reply token.
    // Continue serving other clients while the read is in flight.
    let buffer = device.read(address, len).await?;
    pending.reply_with_buffer(buffer);
}
\end{lstlisting}

The shard's executor polls other tasks while the read completes.

\subsection{Reply token rules}

A reply token is:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Created by the kernel when a call message is enqueued.
    \item Bound to the pending call: it completes exactly that
        caller's future.
    \item Consumable exactly once: a second reply attempt returns
        an error.
    \item Closed by confirmed cancellation. A wait timeout alone leaves the
        pending call and reply authority live, so the caller must keep the owner
        until reply observation or completed cancellation.
    \item Endpoint teardown can complete a call with
        \texttt{IPC\_REPLY\_ENDPOINT\_CLOSED}. The client below maps this
        to its own \texttt{EndpointLost} recovery decision. The kernel
        must finish required loan cleanup before publishing that terminal result, but
        uncertain cleanup can leave the call pending.
    \item The receiving service owns the reply token. It can call a second
        service while holding that authority and the new pending call.
        General reply-token delegation is not exposed by the current API.
\end{itemize}

\section{Connection delegation}

A connection capability can be delegated to another domain with restricted rights.
This is the mechanism by which the userspace name service returns service-level
connections. The following pseudocode illustrates delegation, while the compiling
client API is shown in \autoref{sec:reconnecting-client}:


\begin{lstlisting}[style=rustCode]
// The registrar retains the publisher's mintable connection:
let source = registration_connection.as_ref();
// Ordinary lookup returns SEND | CALL and cannot supply this source.

// After authorizing the client, attenuate its reply:
reply.reply_connection_ref(
    source, IpcRights::SEND | IpcRights::CALL, generation,
)?;
// The client receives SEND | CALL only.
\end{lstlisting}

The kernel mediates. The name service never sees raw endpoint identities, because it
holds the publisher's mintable connection grant. Ordinary lookup removes
minting authority, so a client cannot re-delegate that returned connection.
Each authorized delegation step can only reduce rights. DNS placement lookup
returns catalog metadata, while application authority comes from the local name or
policy grant path.

\section{Service identity and discovery}

The kernel uses opaque endpoint identities. A userspace \textbf{name service}
maps human-readable names to re-delegable connection capabilities:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Registration: a service registers as \texttt{"driver.nic.v1"}
        with a specific endpoint. The name service stores the endpoint's
        connection.
    \item Generation tracking: replacement registrations advance the name's
        generation. A connection continues to identify its original endpoint, so
        re-registration alone does not revoke it or redirect it to a replacement.
        Endpoint death closes that instance's communication path.
    \item Authorization: policy-published services use default-deny rules
        checked against the kernel-authenticated caller identity. Ordinary
        applications acquire a connection through the capability-grant controller
        using their signed deployment descriptor. Bearer access-key lookup is
        retired, and public lookup cannot bypass a protected publication.
    \item Lookup: an application requests a complete name such as
        \texttt{"driver.nic.v1"} and the rights it needs. The owning Rust API
        returns a \texttt{Connection} or an error, so capability handles stay
        inside that owner. No network address or port is needed for local IPC.
\end{itemize}

The name service runs in userspace. The kernel provides the endpoint and
connection primitives, while userspace provides the naming policy.

\subsection{A client with explicit recovery scopes}
\label{sec:reconnecting-client}

The following is a complete, compiling userspace example. It uses the existing
\texttt{greet} service's read-only \texttt{deploy::OP\_GET} protocol so that the
business operation has a concrete implementation. The deployment descriptor
must grant \texttt{CALL} on \texttt{greet}, and the service must be published and
allowed by policy. A NIC client would use its own signed service grant and
versioned protocol in those two places.

\begin{samepage}
Keep the recovery scopes nested in this order:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item The application borrows its launch authority and owns its business
        progress and current operation deadline.
    \item Discovery creates one owned connection for a session.
    \item The session performs business operations through a borrow of that
        connection. A confirmed result advances application progress.
    \item Each operation owns its pending call until reply observation or
        confirmed cancellation. This owner ends before the session connection.
\end{enumerate}
\end{samepage}

The complete source is
\repofile{crates/charlotte-app/examples/reconnecting_client.rs}. It depends on
\texttt{catten-rt}, \texttt{charlotte-app} and
\texttt{charlotte-launch}. The listings below include that source directly, and
the conditional attributes only allow its host tests to use Rust's test harness.

\paragraph*{Application lifetime.}
The example borrows its authority from the launch contract. Before terminal exit
or cooperative shutdown, \texttt{run} must return and its owned resources must
leave scope. If cancellation fails, the example instead aborts with those
owners still in place, as shown below.

\paragraph*{Discovery and session lifetime.}
\texttt{Work} outlives every connection, keeping business progress outside the
session's lifetime. When the session reports endpoint loss,
\texttt{continue} drops that old connection before discovery acquires another.
To bound recovery, the example allows at most three acquired sessions. Exhausting
this limit is distinct from an unstarted operation exhausting its deadline.
Because that deadline belongs to the current business operation and survives
reconnection, repeated discovery cannot renew its budget.
Authorization, resource and malformed-request errors return to application policy.
There is no fallback to public lookup.

\paragraph*{Business operation.}
The inner scope uses the current session. It classifies the current
endpoint-closure status or terminal reply code as \texttt{EndpointLost}, an
application-defined variant rather than a runtime error with that name. Losing
the endpoint does not establish that a replacement is available, and all other
failures return to application policy.

Progress advances and a new deadline begins only after a validated reply. Until
then, shared \texttt{Admission} retries only unstarted queue pressure within the
same deadline, checking lifecycle between attempts. An accepted timeout never
enters that admission retry path.

\paragraph*{Accepted-call lifetime.}
A timeout differs from rejected waiter admission, but both leave the accepted
pending call owned by this scope. Explicit cancellation closes that owner before

This example can repeat an unconfirmed greeting read after endpoint loss because
the operation is read-only. A write, payment, device command or other mutation
needs its own recovery contract: for example a durable request ID, deduplication
and a query for the recorded outcome. Successful cancellation does not undo an
effect already performed by the service. Timeout, cancelled replies and rejected
wait admission therefore stop this example, and they never enter its reconnect path.
Its progress counter survives connection replacement within this process only.
Durable application progress belongs outside the session and needs its own commit
and recovery rules.

The single-call wait suits a dedicated client that has no inbound work needed to
produce the reply. A service reactor must bind the pending call and its other
interests to one CQ and continue serving them. For the waiting contract, see
\repofile{docs/reference/cq-wait.md}. Shutdown is checked between acquisitions
and calls, so an accepted RPC may occupy the observation budget before that check.
Grant acquisition still uses bounded availability backoff because applications
have no publication-readiness subscription. It returns authorization and other
rejections immediately. This example demonstrates ownership and recovery policy, but
it does not establish production readiness of the operating system.

\section{Two kernel data paths}

A central distinction is that endpoint IPC is for \textbf{cross-domain
service calls}. Completion queues are for \textbf{kernel and device operations}.

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{|l|l|}
\hline
\textbf{Endpoint IPC path} & \textbf{Completion queue path} \\
\hline
Application $\rightarrow$ network service & Timer expiry \\
Network service $\rightarrow$ NIC driver & DMA completion \\
Application $\rightarrow$ filesystem service & IRQ-derived device completion \\
Service $\rightarrow$ name service & Waiting for thread/process exit \\
\hline
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

A userspace driver may compose both: receive an endpoint message (data path), then
submit a hardware operation and await a CQ entry (control path). The endpoint
message and the hardware completion are related \emph{in the service's logic}, but
they are different kernel abstractions with different behavior.

\section{Authority, concurrency, and restart}

\begin{enumerate}
    \item Communication carries authority: a client that
        holds a connection capability was authorized (at grant time, by the name
        service or policy service) to communicate with the target. Every message
        is authorized by possession of a kernel-mediated capability rather than
        by an address or UID alone.

    \item Scalar and memory messages are distinct: the kernel does not
        copy arbitrary data between address spaces by default. Small control
        messages stay small. Bulk data moves as memory objects
        (\autoref{chap:memory-objects}) with
        explicit ownership rules.

    \item Deferred replies enable efficient concurrency: a server is never
        forced to block waiting for one slow client. It holds reply tokens for
        in-flight work and continues serving other requests. The shard's executor
        multiplexes naturally.

    \item Endpoint loss is observable: clients classify the endpoint-closure
        status and terminal reply, as in \autoref{sec:reconnecting-client}.
        A replacement cannot inherit the old instance's reply tokens.

    \item The kernel provides endpoints and connections. The userspace name
        service decides who can register what name,
        who can look up what service, and which rights policy permits. The
        kernel remains small, and policy is replaceable without a kernel rebuild.
\end{enumerate}
