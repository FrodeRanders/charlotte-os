\chapter{Endpoints: Communication as a First-Class Primitive}
\label{chap:endpoints}

An endpoint answers: \textbf{``Who am I communicating with?''} It is
CharlotteOS's primary primitive for calls between protection domains.

\section{Server and connection model}

An \textbf{endpoint} is a bounded server-side IPC receive object. A service creates
one and receives messages on it. A \textbf{connection capability} is a client-side
authority to send to that endpoint. Connections are minted from the endpoint
(typically by the name service) and delegated to clients.

\begin{verbatim}
Service creates endpoint:   endpoint_create(interface="ns", capacity=16)
Name service mints conn:    connection_mint(endpoint, SEND | CALL)
Client receives conn:       name_service.lookup("ns") -> ConnectionCap
\end{verbatim}

Ordinary clients do not mint arbitrary connections. The name service or a policy
service normally delegates them after an access check.

\subsection{Namespace publication}

Root registration prepares the IPC namespace node and both budget accounts
fallibly before taking publication guards. It captures an exact-generation
root lease, releases lifecycle/table guards, then links that prepared storage
under IPC alone. Cleanup cannot pass the retained lease during this handoff.
Missing or retired namespaces reject; ordinary IPC never recreates them.

After fenced loan and capability drain, cleanup detaches the empty namespace
node and disposes it outside IPC. Outstanding delegated endpoint/record charges
keep their original accounts through ASID reuse. This covers namespace storage;
per-capability and endpoint map allocations, account byte/principal
admission and wider destructor contexts remain audit work. See
\repofile|docs/reference/ipc-namespace-publication.md|.

\subsection{Endpoint storage admission}

Endpoint records and queue backing have generation-scoped sponsorship. The
current kernel admits at most 64 endpoint records and 8,192 queue slots per
domain, and 1,024 records and 32,768 slots per node. Ordinary domains share
768 records and 24,576 slots; the remainder is available to kernel-designated
platform domains. These are initial kernel policies, independent of socket
capacity, rather than deployment-descriptor settings.

A single queue admits at most 4,096 messages. Its backing is preallocated
fallibly after reserving slots, rounded to a power of two with a minimum of
four. Growth stages a second charged queue before moving messages, so failed
growth preserves the existing queue. Shrinking reduces message admission but
retains the backing and its charge until it is freed.

Owner closure drains the queue and frees its backing. Delegated connections
can retain the closed endpoint record; its original generation pays for that
record until the last reference disappears. Late cleanup after ASID reuse
credits only the old generation.

\subsection{Connection, call and reply record admission}

Each sponsoring namespace generation can retain 512 connection capabilities,
512 pending calls and 512 outstanding reply tokens. Each dimension has a node
ceiling of 8,192 and an ordinary-domain share of 6,144. These initial kernel
policies are separate from endpoint queue capacity and waiter limits.

The grantor sponsors direct grants and re-delegations. The requesting caller
sponsors both its pending call and the server's reply token, and pays for a
connection returned in that solicited reply. Repeated lookups therefore cannot
consume the serving grantor's connection allowance. A recipient can hold
connections sponsored by several namespaces; 512 is not its total
capability-table limit. Sponsorship does not change capability authority.
Completed and observed calls remain charged until closed, while reply-token
charges return on reply or cancellation.

Calls reject invalid handles and unavailable queues before preparing record
storage, then recheck them before mutation. They retain their exact caller root
and reserve both record counts atomically
before preparing record storage, waiter metadata or attachments. Pending and
reply-token nodes allocate fallibly outside IPC and publish by relinking. Their containing
owner explicitly finishes unused preparation after IPC unlock, root last;
abandonment retains every field. Reply, close and confirmed cancellation carry detached record nodes outside IPC
and free payload/storage before refunding the original record charge. Scalar
completion retains its detached notification batch with the node until explicit
finish; fallback retains both. Claim sources use the original token
classification through promotion and ASID reuse. See \repofile|docs/reference/ipc-record-budgets.md|.

Connection-bearing calls and replies admit their
connection before copying memory, consuming the token or revoking a loan.
Kernel rejection leaves the inputs intact. The consuming Rust
\texttt{ReplyToken::reply\_connection*} methods close/cancel their token on an
error and preserve the borrowed grant source; they do not return a retryable
token.

Teardown fences admission before taking its capability snapshot. Receive and
connection-publication paths reject a retiring namespace, preventing late
capabilities from escaping that snapshot. Charges retain the original
generation after delegation or ASID reuse. Endpoint and capability insertion,
list control blocks, other capability kinds and comprehensive kernel heap
accounting remain incomplete.

\subsection{Receive and reply waiter admission}

Threads waiting for endpoint readiness or a pending-call reply use removable,
generation-sponsored registrations. Each source links at most 64 entries. They share
the completion/CQ waiter pools: 1,024 entries per waiting domain, 8,192 per node
and an ordinary share of 6,144. Sponsorship follows the waiting thread, not the
endpoint owner. Admission precedes scheduler parking; message or reply state
that is already ready returns without retaining an entry.

Ready admission or thread reaping cancels the owning registration. IPC detaches
message, reply and source-closure notifications under its registry lock and
invokes them only after releasing that lock. Endpoint closure can combine
receiver and queued-call batches without allocating a callback vector. Entries
remain charged until their detached storage is actually freed.
Detached batches no longer occupy the source's linked count; they still occupy
the shared domain/node pools while a reusable readiness list is rearmed.

Untimed receive and reply waits retry cooperatively when admission is full. A
reply wait preserves the pending call and its memory loan until reply or explicit
cancellation revokes receiver access. The existing Rust owners need no manual
unregister step. Kernel timed reply waits can return without a reply on deadline
or admission rejection; neither event by itself ends a loan.

Readiness lists and pending-call lists are prepared fallibly before source
publication. Call preparation precedes memory transfer, lending and delegated
connection attachments, so failure at this stage leaves those inputs untouched.
Connection, reply-token and pending-call counts use the separate record budgets
above. List control blocks and other kernel metadata still need comprehensive
admission and fallible allocation.

\section{Message classes}

The ABI distinguishes two message classes:

\subsection{Scalar messages}

A scalar message carries an opcode, one 64-bit argument word, and optionally a
reply token. It fits in registers. It is suitable for:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Opcodes and request types.
    \item Capability handles.
    \item Status values and queue indices.
    \item Compact control-plane operations where the payload is a single word.
\end{itemize}

Scalar messages require no memory-object allocation, page-table change, or TLB
invalidation.

\subsection{Memory-bearing messages}

A message can carry one or more \textbf{memory-object attachments} with explicit
transfer modes (see \autoref{chap:memory-objects}). These are suitable for:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Structured requests and responses.
    \item Strings, names, and configuration structures.
    \item Packet payloads and block I/O buffers.
    \item Larger replies that exceed register width.
\end{itemize}

The kernel validates each attachment: the sender must hold the referenced memory
object with sufficient rights for the declared transfer mode. The receiver gets a
new capability to the memory (or a temporary mapping, for borrows).

\section{Message envelope}

The kernel validates a fixed-width message envelope. It does not understand Rust
enums, \texttt{serde} formats, or protocol-specific types:

\begin{lstlisting}[style=rustCode]
struct MessageHeader {
    interface: u128,       // protocol identity
    version: u32,
    opcode: u32,
    flags: u32,
    inline_len: u16,
    segment_count: u16,
    capability_count: u16,
    reserved: u16,
    user_tag: u64,
}
\end{lstlisting}

Constraints on the ABI:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Fixed-width fields: no \texttt{usize}, no pointer-sized values.
    \item Explicit alignment: no compiler-dependent layout.
    \item Checked offsets and lengths: the kernel rejects out-of-bounds.
    \item Explicit protocol versioning: forward compatibility is negotiated.
    \item Maximum attachment counts: bounded kernel work per message.
    \item No arbitrary userspace pointers as durable message identity.
\end{itemize}

\section{Call and reply}

The most common IPC pattern is \textbf{call/reply}: a client sends a request and
expects a response.

\subsection{Synchronous-looking futures}

At the Rust level, a client writes:


\begin{lstlisting}[style=rustCode]
let response = network_service.connect(address).await?;
\end{lstlisting}

Underneath, the runtime:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item Sends a call message through the connection capability.
    \item The kernel creates a one-shot \textbf{reply token} bound to this call.
    \item The caller task becomes pending (the \texttt{Future} returns
        \texttt{Poll::Pending}).
    \item The caller shard continues running other tasks: no thread is
        dedicated to this request.
    \item The server receives the message on its endpoint.
    \item The server replies (immediately or later).
    \item Reply arrival wakes the client future, which returns
        \texttt{Poll::Ready(response)}.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Deferred replies}

A server may retain a reply token and complete it later while continuing to
serve other requests on the same shard. This supports device-driven
operations:


\begin{lstlisting}[style=rustCode]
async fn handle_read(pending: PendingRequest) {
    // Submit a DMA read, retaining the reply token.
    // Continue serving other clients while the read is in flight.
    let buffer = device.read(address, len).await?;
    pending.reply_with_buffer(buffer);
}
\end{lstlisting}

The shard's executor polls other tasks while the read completes.

\subsection{Reply token semantics}

A reply token is:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Created by the kernel when a call message is enqueued.
    \item Bound to the pending call: it completes exactly that
        caller's future.
    \item Consumable exactly once: a second reply attempt returns
        an error.
    \item Closed by confirmed cancellation. A wait timeout alone leaves the
        pending call and reply authority live; the caller must retain the owner
        until reply observation or completed cancellation.
    \item Endpoint teardown can complete a call with
        \texttt{IPC\_REPLY\_ENDPOINT\_CLOSED}. The client below maps this
        to its own \texttt{ServiceRestarted} recovery decision. The kernel
        must finish required loan cleanup before publishing that terminal result;
        uncertain cleanup can leave the call pending.
    \item The receiving service owns the reply token. It can call a second
        service while retaining that authority and the new pending call.
        General reply-token delegation is not exposed by the current API.
\end{itemize}

\section{Connection delegation}

A connection capability can be delegated to another domain with attenuated rights.
This is the mechanism by which the userspace name service returns service-level
connections. The following pseudocode illustrates delegation; the compiling
client API is shown in \autoref{sec:reconnecting-client}:


\begin{lstlisting}[style=rustCode]
// The registrar retains the publisher's mintable connection:
let source = registration_connection.as_ref();
// Ordinary lookup returns SEND | CALL and cannot supply this source.

// After authorizing the client, attenuate its reply:
reply.reply_connection_ref(
    source, IpcRights::SEND | IpcRights::CALL, generation,
)?;
// The client receives SEND | CALL only.
\end{lstlisting}

The kernel mediates. The name service never sees raw endpoint identities; it
holds the publisher's mintable connection grant. Ordinary lookup removes
minting authority, so a client cannot re-delegate that returned connection.
Each authorized delegation step can only reduce rights. DNS placement lookup
returns catalog metadata; application authority comes from the local name or
policy grant path.

\section{Service identity and discovery}

The kernel uses opaque endpoint identities. A userspace \textbf{name service}
maps human-readable names to re-delegable connection capabilities:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Registration: a service registers as \texttt{"driver.nic.v1"}
        with a specific endpoint. The name service stores the endpoint's
        connection.
    \item Generation tracking: replacement registrations advance the name's
        generation. A connection continues to identify its original endpoint;
        re-registration alone does not revoke it or redirect it to a replacement.
        Endpoint death closes that instance's communication path.
    \item Authorization: policy-published services use default-deny rules
        checked against the kernel-authenticated caller identity. Ordinary
        applications acquire a connection through the capability-grant controller
        using their signed deployment descriptor. Bearer access-key lookup is
        retired; public lookup cannot bypass a protected publication.
    \item Lookup: an application requests a complete name such as
        \texttt{"driver.nic.v1"} and the rights it needs. The owning Rust API
        returns a \texttt{Connection} or an error; capability handles stay
        inside that owner. No network address or port is needed for local IPC.
\end{itemize}

The name service runs in userspace. The kernel provides the endpoint and
connection primitives; userspace provides the naming policy.

\subsection{A client with explicit recovery scopes}
\label{sec:reconnecting-client}

The following is a complete, compiling userspace example. It uses the existing
\texttt{greet} service's read-only \texttt{deploy::OP\_GET} protocol so that the
business operation has a concrete implementation. The deployment descriptor
must grant \texttt{CALL} on \texttt{greet}, and the service must be published and
allowed by policy. A NIC client would use its own signed service grant and
versioned protocol in those two places.

\begin{samepage}
Keep the recovery scopes nested in this order:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item The application borrows its launch authority and owns its business
        progress and current operation deadline.
    \item Discovery creates one owned connection for a session.
    \item The session performs business operations through a borrow of that
        connection. A confirmed result advances application progress.
    \item Each operation owns its pending call until reply observation or
        confirmed cancellation. This owner ends before the session connection.
\end{enumerate}
\end{samepage}

The complete source is
\repofile{crates/catten-services/examples/reconnecting_client.rs}. It depends on
\texttt{catten-rt}, \texttt{catten-services}, \texttt{catten-syscall} and
\texttt{charlotte-launch}. The listings below include that source directly;
the conditional attributes only allow its host tests to use Rust's test harness.

\paragraph*{Application lifetime.}
The launch contract supplies borrowed authority. Terminal exit and cooperative
shutdown happen after \texttt{run} has returned and its owned resources have
left scope. Failed cancellation has a separate retaining abort path below.

\lstinputlisting[style=rustCode,linerange={APPBEGIN-APPEND},rangeprefix={//},includerangemarker=false]{../../crates/catten-services/examples/reconnecting_client.rs}

\paragraph*{Discovery and session lifetime.}
\texttt{Work} outlives every connection. When the session reports endpoint loss,
\texttt{continue} drops that old connection before discovery acquires another.
There are at most three sessions. The deadline belongs to the current business
operation and survives reconnection; repeated discovery cannot renew its budget.
Authorization, resource and malformed-request errors return to application policy.
There is no fallback to public lookup.

\lstinputlisting[style=rustCode,linerange={DISCOVERYBEGIN-DISCOVERYEND},rangeprefix={//},includerangemarker=false]{../../crates/catten-services/examples/reconnecting_client.rs}

\paragraph*{Business operation.}
The inner scope contains only use of this session. Its \texttt{ServiceRestarted}
variant is an application-defined classification of the current endpoint-closure
status or terminal reply code. The runtime does not export an error variant with
that name, and closure alone does not establish that a replacement is available.
All other failures step out to the application. Progress and the next deadline
advance only after a validated reply.

\lstinputlisting[style=rustCode,linerange={SESSIONBEGIN-SESSIONEND},rangeprefix={//},includerangemarker=false]{../../crates/catten-services/examples/reconnecting_client.rs}

\paragraph*{Accepted-call lifetime.}
A timeout and rejected waiter admission are different outcomes. Both leave an
accepted pending call owned here. Explicit cancellation closes that owner before
returning an error; rejected cancellation returns the owner, which stays live
through terminal domain abort. Cancellation can wait for loan cleanup, so the
five-second observation budget is not a guaranteed teardown deadline.

\lstinputlisting[style=rustCode,linerange={CALLBEGIN-CALLEND},rangeprefix={//},includerangemarker=false]{../../crates/catten-services/examples/reconnecting_client.rs}

This example can repeat an unconfirmed greeting read after endpoint loss because
the operation is read-only. A write, payment, device command or other mutation
needs its own recovery contract: for example a durable request ID, deduplication
and a query for the recorded outcome. Successful cancellation does not undo an
effect already performed by the service. Timeout, cancelled replies and rejected
wait admission therefore stop this example; they never enter its reconnect path.
Its progress counter survives connection replacement within this process only.
Durable application progress belongs outside the session and needs its own commit
and recovery rules.

The single-call wait suits a dedicated client that has no inbound work needed to
produce the reply. A service reactor must bind the pending call and its other
interests to one CQ and continue serving them; see
\repofile{docs/reference/cq-wait.md}. Shutdown is checked between acquisitions
and calls, so an accepted RPC may occupy the observation budget before that check.
Grant acquisition still uses bounded availability backoff because applications
have no publication-readiness subscription. It returns authorization and other
rejections immediately. This example demonstrates ownership and recovery policy;
it does not establish production readiness of the operating system.

\section{Two kernel data paths}

A central distinction is that endpoint IPC is for \textbf{cross-domain
service calls}. Completion queues are for \textbf{kernel and device operations}.

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{|l|l|}
\hline
\textbf{Endpoint IPC path} & \textbf{Completion queue path} \\
\hline
Application $\rightarrow$ network service & Timer expiry \\
Network service $\rightarrow$ NIC driver & DMA completion \\
Application $\rightarrow$ filesystem service & IRQ-derived device completion \\
Service $\rightarrow$ name service & Waiting for thread/process exit \\
\hline
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

A userspace driver may compose both: receive an endpoint message (data path), then
submit a hardware operation and await a CQ entry (control path). The endpoint
message and the hardware completion are related \emph{in the service's logic}, but
they are different kernel abstractions with different semantics.

\section{Authority, concurrency, and restart}

\begin{enumerate}
    \item Communication carries authority: a client that
        holds a connection capability was authorized (at grant time, by the name
        service or policy service) to communicate with the target. Every message
        is authorized by possession of a kernel-mediated capability rather than
        by an address or UID alone.

    \item Scalar and memory messages are distinct: the kernel does not
        copy arbitrary data between address spaces by default. Small control
        messages stay small. Bulk data moves as memory objects
        (\autoref{chap:memory-objects}) with
        explicit ownership semantics.

    \item Deferred replies enable efficient concurrency: a server is never
        forced to block waiting for one slow client. It retains reply tokens for
        in-flight work and continues serving other requests. The shard's executor
        multiplexes naturally.

    \item Endpoint loss is observable: clients classify the endpoint-closure
        status and terminal reply, as in \autoref{sec:reconnecting-client}.
        A replacement cannot inherit the old instance's reply tokens.

    \item The kernel provides endpoints and connections. The userspace name
        service decides who can register what name,
        who can look up what service, and which rights policy permits. The
        kernel remains small; policy is replaceable without a kernel rebuild.
\end{enumerate}
